# Whose words? Socrates as narrator — notes for the speaker rulings

Status: first pass, 2026-11-16. The texts are quoted from our TLG Greek and checked. The
secondary works are marked by how far they were checked: **read** means I read the
argument itself; **located** means only that the work exists and covers this, from catalogue,
abstract, or someone else's citation.

## The question

When Socrates (or Phaedo, Apollodorus, Cephalus) narrates, what he reports others saying
can be read two ways:

- **as the narrator's voice**: his memory, selection, and paraphrase, shaped by his point of
  view (the Thrasymachus of Book I is Socrates' Thrasymachus);
- **as evidence of the others' words**: the narration is a transparent vehicle, and direct
  speech is the speaker's own.

For us this decides what a search for "Thrasymachus" should mean in a narrated work, and
whether reported voices belong to the narrator too (rulings 1 and 2 in
`narrated-speakers-plan.md`).

## What Plato's own texts say

**Plato's own theory of narration, Republic 3, 392d–394c.** Socrates divides all
storytelling into ἁπλῇ διηγήσει, διὰ μιμήσεως, or both (392d). When Homer gives Chryses' speech, he speaks
ὥσπερ αὐτὸς ὢν ὁ Χρύσης and tries to make us think μὴ Ὅμηρον … εἶναι τὸν λέγοντα ἀλλὰ τὸν ἱερέα
(393a–b). In direct speech the narrator ὁμοιοῦν … τὴν αὑτοῦ λέξιν ἑκάστῳ ὃν ἂν προείπῃ ὡς
ἐροῦντα (393c). That is imitation, his own voice made over into the speaker's. Socrates then retells the Chryses scene without
imitation (393d–394a), and the speeches turn into reported speech in the poet's own voice.
The Republic is itself the "mixed" kind it describes. On Plato's own terms, then:
- direct speech is the narrator *taking on* the speaker's voice: presented as the speaker's
  words, and still the narrator's performance;
- narration and indirect report are the narrator speaking as himself.

**Narrators who say they have condensed or shaped.**
- Republic 350c–d: Thrasymachus agreed to all this, Socrates says, οὐχ ὡς ἐγὼ νῦν ῥᾳδίως λέγω, ἀλλ'
  ἑλκόμενος καὶ μόγις, μετὰ ἱδρῶτος. The narrator himself flags that his summary smooths
  over what was said.
- Symposium 178a: πάντων μὲν οὖν ἃ ἕκαστος εἶπεν, οὔτε πάνυ ὁ Ἀριστόδημος ἐμέμνητο οὔτ' αὖ
  ἐγὼ ἃ ἐκεῖνος ἔλεγε πάντα. The speeches are selected (ἃ … ἔδοξέ μοι ἀξιομνημόνευτον).
- Symposium 173b: yet Apollodorus checked parts with Socrates, καί μοι ὡμολόγει καθάπερ
  ἐκεῖνος διηγεῖτο, a claim to authenticity.
- Phaedo 58d, 59c: Phaedo will try (πειράσομαι) to tell it all ἐξ ἀρχῆς; he presents
  memory, not transcript.
- Parmenides 126c: Antiphon heard the conversation many times from Pythodorus and as a
  youth learned it thoroughly (διεμελέτησεν). The chain is long, and its fidelity is stressed.

**A narration taken out again.** Theaetetus 143b–c: Euclides wrote the conversation down
not διηγούμενον ὡς διηγεῖτο but as dialogue, dropping the αἱ μεταξὺ τῶν λόγων διηγήσεις
("καὶ ἐγὼ ἔφην", "συνέφη", "οὐχ ὡμολόγει"). Plato shows narrated and dramatic form as
interchangeable for the same exchange. The narrating layer is something a writer can add or
remove.

## Scholarship found so far

**Read:**
- Thomas A. Schmitz, "Der vermittelte Bericht in Platons Symposion," *Würzburger Jahrbücher
  für die Altertumswissenschaft* 20 (1994/95). The nested frame **vouches for the
  authenticity of what is reported, not for its detail** ("bürgt für die Authentizität des
  Berichteten, nicht jedoch für Detailgenauigkeit"). The unreliable report of the anonymous
  ἄλλος τις (172b) is set aside for Apollodorus' account, which Socrates confirms (173b).
  Schmitz disagrees with Clay on this.

**Located, not yet read:**
- Stephen Halliwell, "Diegesis – Mimesis," *Living Handbook of Narratology* (Hamburg), on
  Republic 392d–394c and Theaetetus 143b–c. Seen only through a search summary.
- Anne-Marie Schultz, *Plato's Socrates as Narrator: A Philosophical Muse* (Lexington, 2013;
  pbk 2015). The only book-length study of exactly this, covering the Lysis, Charmides,
  Protagoras, Euthydemus, and Republic. Its blurb says it reads Socrates' **narrative commentary** (his
  asides on his own and others' states) as a guide to his philosophical practice. This should
  be read first.
- Kathryn A. Morgan, "Plato," ch. 28 in de Jong, Nünlist & Bowie (eds.), *Narrators,
  Narratees, and Narratives in Ancient Greek Literature* (Brill, 2004), 357ff. A
  narratological survey of the dialogues. It is open access on OAPEN, but the site's bot check
  stopped the download.
- Diskin Clay, "Plato's First Words," *Yale Classical Studies* 29 (1992) 113–29. Cited by
  Schmitz for the view that "the Symposium is the best example of a frame dialogue related by
  unreliable narrators" (125 n. 24).
- David M. Halperin, "Plato and the Erotics of Narrativity," in Klagge & Smith (eds.),
  *Methods of Interpreting Plato and His Dialogues* (OSAP suppl., 1992) 93–129. On the
  nested Symposium frames.
- William A. Johnson, "Dramatic Frame and Philosophic Idea in Plato," *AJP* 119 (1998)
  577–98.
- Hans Reynen, "Der vermittelte Bericht im Platonischen Symposion," *Gymnasium* 74 (1967)
  405–22.
- Ruby Blondell, *The Play of Character in Plato's Dialogues* (CUP, 2002). On
  characterization generally; its contents list no chapter on narration.
- Ancient background: Diogenes Laertius 3.50 records the dramatic / narrative / mixed
  classification of the dialogues.

## What this means for the rulings

The texts support the line the rulings already draw, and they mark where it is soft:

1. **Direct speech as the speaker's, narration and indirect report as the narrator's**
   (rulings 1 and 2) is Plato's own distinction at Republic 392d–394c. That is a principled
   basis, not just a convenience.
2. **But direct speech in a narrated work is the narrator's mimesis** (393c). Its fidelity is
   claimed in some frames (Symposium 173b, Parmenides 126c) and qualified in others (Republic
   350d, Symposium 178a). That is the reliability question, and no labelling settles it. It
   is a reading choice.
3. So the search could offer what the data already supports: a speaker filter that means
   **"words given to Thrasymachus"**, plus a scope for **"everything in Socrates'
   narration"**, the whole of a work he narrates, every voice included. A reader who holds
   that all of it is Socrates' Thrasymachus can then search it that way. Every segment
   already records its narrator and, for a quoted voice, who quotes it, so this needs no
   change to the run, only to search and its wording.
