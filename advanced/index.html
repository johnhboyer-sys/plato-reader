A guide

Advanced search

Tools for asking questions of Plato's Greek, and of its translations, that a plain word search cannot answer. You need no Greek to use most of them, and you can read the sections in any order. Every number on this page is counted from the texts this site serves: 36 dialogues, 561,531 Greek words. Check any of them against the search itself.

First, two things worth knowing

Greek changes the ends of words. English mostly does not: "virtue" looks the same wherever it stands. Greek changes the ending to show the word's job — ἀρετή, ἀρετῆς, ἀρετήν are one word doing three jobs. So a search for one spelling misses most of the places that word occurs. Several of these tools exist to fix that.

You can type Greek in plain letters. With no Greek keyboard, type the sounds instead — scholars call this Beta Code. texnh gives τέχνη. The search page has a key to it, and this page shows examples both ways.

Numbers like 34b are Stephanus pages, the standard way of citing Plato. Most editions print them in the margin, so the same number finds the same passage in any edition or translation. Results here use them throughout, and nothing finer: Plato is cited by page and section, never by line.

ΑWildcards

Use this when you know how a word begins but not how it ends.

End a word with * and it stands for whatever follows. fron* reaches 68 different words across the dialogues — φρόνησις, φρόνιμος, φρονεῖν and the rest of that family at once. Since Greek marks a word's job at the end, one wildcard gathers the whole paradigm.

A wildcard reaches further the more text you give it. In the Republic alone, l* reaches 302 different words; across all 36 dialogues it reaches 891, out of 46,389 distinct spellings in the corpus. The search reports occurrences rather than distinct words, so it answers l* with passages, not with a vocabulary count.

A star can also stand inside a word, and ? stands for exactly one letter. l*s is every word that begins with λ and ends with ς — 228 different spellings — and log?s finds λόγος but not λόγους, which has two letters in that place. The pattern is matched against the whole word, so a star in the middle never quietly becomes "every word beginning λ".

A star at the start of a Greek word is read differently: in Beta Code it marks a capital letter, so *swkraths is simply Σωκράτης, not a wildcard. In English, where there is no such marker, a leading star is a wildcard like any other: *ness finds happiness and goodness. If you want every form of one dictionary word, do not reach for a wildcard at all — use Single lemma, or set the Greek box to Any form of this word and type the form you have.

ΒSingle lemma

Use this when you want one dictionary word, in every form, and nothing else.

Open Advanced search and start typing a word under Single lemma. A list appears of the dictionary words that begin that way — the headword, a short gloss and how often it occurs — and you pick the one you mean. The search then finds every form of that word and no other: no spelling that merely looks like it, and no other word that one of its forms could also belong to. The list has 12,907 entries, one for every word the search can find.

Typing ψυχ offers ψυχή first, as the commonest, then the other words that begin that way, such as ψυχρός, so you choose rather than guess. Pick more than one and the search asks for any of them. The same list serves a Word in any of its forms term under Words near each other.

The search ignores accents, so a few words that differ only by an accent — ὅρος and ὄρος — are one choice in the list, and the list says that it cannot separate them.

ΓWord endings

Use this when you know a phrase but want every form of it, not just the one you typed.

Since Greek changes word endings, an exact phrase search finds only the version you typed. Search two or more Greek words and a button appears beside the count: Find this phrase in any inflection. It works out which dictionary word each of your words belongs to, then looks for every combination of those words in the order you gave.

The phrase index shows the same gathering, and you can see the size of it there. τὸ καλόν ("the beautiful") stands 93 times as those exact letters, in 14 dialogues. Read as dictionary words — ὁ καλός, which gathers τοῦ καλοῦ, τῷ καλῷ, τῶν καλῶν and the rest — it stands 385 times, in 32. To find those yourself you would have to know that τό, τοῦ and τῷ are one word. The tool knows it for you.

It invents nothing. πάνυ μὲν οὖν ("certainly") stands 333 times as written and 333 times read as dictionary words: those three words take one form only, so there is nothing to gather. A tool that always found more would tell you nothing.

ΔEnglish searches

Use this to find a word in the translations, in any of its forms.

An English search finds a word in all its forms by default: a plural or possessive finds its singular, and back. In the translations here "soul" stands 1,073 times, and "souls" and "soul's" 198 times more; Any form of this word finds them all, and Only as I typed it the one spelling you gave. Irregular plurals are known too: "man" finds "men", "statesman" finds "statesmen".

A phrase is matched as whole words in a row, so "the good" does not find "they" or "goodness". A hyphenated word, "self-control", is found as its words standing together, and a word printed with an accent is found when you type plain letters.

Every translation

11 dialogues carry Benjamin Jowett's translation beside the Loeb. An English search asks each translation separately, so "all words" means all in one translator's prose, and a result shows the passage in each translation that has the words, under its translator's name. The Translations row above the results counts the hits in each and narrows the results to one; the Works row does the same for the dialogues. A Jowett passage is placed at the section where its speech begins, since his translation is matched to Plato's speeches, not to the Stephanus sections.

A search is written into the address bar as you run it, so you can bookmark it, send it to someone, or step back through your searches with the browser's back button.

ΕSearch by grammar

Use this to find a kind of word rather than a particular word.

Every Greek word here carries a grammatical description: singular or plural, past or present, and so on. You can search those descriptions instead of the spelling. Pick as many as you like; a word must match them all.

Grammar is one term inside a proximity search, never a search of its own. On its own it would not be a question: there are 19,702 genitive plural feminines in the dialogues, and a list of them tells you something about Greek rather than about Plato. Beside another term it earns its keep — this word near a verb in the optative is a real question, and it is the one the tool answers. Set a term's Kind to Grammatical description under Words near each other.

You need not know how any of these forms are spelled. You pick the description; the search finds the words.

Why some results say "one of several readings"

The same Greek letters can be two different forms. When that happens the tool marks the result instead of choosing one for you.

Plato's dialogues hold 7,212 optatives. Of those, 4,054 have one possible reading; a hit on any of the rest comes back marked, because you could read those letters another way. The mark shows no flaw in the search. It reports a fact about the Greek.

A clearer case: of the 473 genitive duals (the dual describes pairs of things), not one is certain. In the dual, genitive and dative share one ending. The search cannot tell them apart because Greek does not, and it says so instead of deciding for you.

You will find no option for "noun", "verb" or "adjective". We left it out on purpose. The descriptions this site uses do not record parts of speech, and we cannot work them out — a participle behaves as both verb and noun, and nothing separates a noun from an adjective. Offering the choice would mean making it up.

ϚWords near each other

Use this to find where two ideas are discussed together.

Ask for two or more things close together in the text. Each term takes a word as written, a word in all its forms (picked from the same list as Single lemma), an exact phrase, or a grammatical description, and you can mix them. "This word near a verb in the optative" is a question you can put here. Search either word on its own and you get every passage holding it; ask for both together and you get the passages where Plato is doing something with the pair.

The controls

  • Window — how close is close. Five words by default. You can widen it to 50.
  • Measured in — count that distance in words, or ask instead for the same line of Greek or the same speech. A speech here is one turn: everything one speaker says before the next answers. That is the unit a dialogue is jointed at, and below the book it is the only division Plato's text has.
  • Relative to term 1 — you can require a term to come before or after the first one, rather than simply near it.
  • In this order — everything must appear in the order you typed it.

A match never crosses from one book into the next, however wide the window: the last words of one book and the first of the next are not near each other in any useful sense. Matches do cross from one speaker's turn into the next by default — a question and its answer belong together — but you can turn that off.

ΖWho is speaking

Use this to hear one voice at a time.

A dialogue gives every word a speaker, and the Greek text marks each change of speaker. So a search can be held to one mouth: ἀρετή as Socrates uses it, and separately as everyone else does. Open Spoken by on the search page, choose Only these speakers or Anyone but these, and tick the names. The cast offered is read from the works you have selected, most talkative first, with the number of speeches beside each name. The filter applies to every kind of search on the page — the plain boxes, words near each other, and a phrase in any inflection — and each Greek hit is then labelled with its speaker.

Across the 25 works that carry speaker labels there are 11,827 speeches by 43 named speakers; 3,924 of those speeches are Socrates'.

What it holds to, and what it cannot

  • A Greek hit is held to the word: it is kept only if the speech it stands in belongs to a chosen speaker.
  • An English hit is held to the passage. The translation is aligned to the Stephanus section, not to the word, so an English match is kept when someone you chose speaks anywhere in that section. Expect the English side to be a little generous.
  • In Words near each other, every term must fall in a chosen speaker's speech — Socrates' ἀρετή beside Protagoras' ἐπιστήμη is not a match for "only Socrates".
  • The narrated works (Apology, Charmides, Letters, Lovers, Republic) carry no speaker labels in Burnet's text — the narrator reports who spoke inside the prose — so nothing in them can be attributed. A filtered search leaves them out and says so under the results. Anyone but Socrates does not admit them either: a word nobody is marked as saying is not known to be someone else's.
  • The reported dialogues (Euthydemus (Socrates), Lysis (Socrates), Parmenides (Cephalus), Phaedo (Phaedo), Protagoras (Socrates), Symposium (Apollodorus)) are labelled only at the frame — the narrator and whoever asked for the story — so every word of the conversation inside would count as the narrator's. They are left out too, and named under the results.
  • Words before a work's first speaker label have no speaker and fail both modes.

The speaker names are the ones the reader prints beside each turn, taken from the OCT sigla. Where a translation names a speaker differently ("Meno's Boy" for ΠΑΙ.), it is the reader's name that appears here.

ΗThe phrase index

Use this when you do not yet know what to look for.

Search answers "where does this occur?" The phrase index answers a different question: what does Plato repeat? It lists every phrase of two to five words that appears at least twice anywhere in the dialogues — 84,894 of them as written, 219,287 when forms of the same word count together, and 187,170 in the English translations. (If you have met the term, these are what linguists call n-grams.)

This matters because a dialogue is built as much out of set phrases as out of single words, and no word search will show them to you: ἦν δ’ ἐγώ ("said I", Socrates narrating) occurs 860 times across 11 dialogues; πάνυ μὲν οὖν ("certainly") 333 times; ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν ("so to speak") 74 times in 20. Not one of those is a word. Each is a habit of thought, and browsing is the only way to meet them.

Reading a row

Every row is one phrase and four numbers. This is the row for ἦν δ’ ἐγώ:

The same phrase, counted two ways

The first two buttons at the top of the page are the same corpus read differently, and it is worth seeing what changes. Word as written counts the letters on the page: change one ending and it is a different row. Word in any of its forms counts the words behind the letters, so every inflection of the phrase gathers into a single row. Either takes the phrase as it stands on the page — type ἦν δ’ ἐγώ and the second finds it under ἠμί δέ ἐγώ, which is where it is stored, and tells you it read the words that way.

The dictionary row is never the smaller of the two: it gathers the formula's other endings in with it. It also asks more of you: the same dictionary words can be assembled into phrases that are not the one you wanted, and the tool cannot tell the difference. Use it to discover what you would otherwise miss, then read as written to see what you have.

The score changes between the two rows for what is, in the main, the same expression. Nothing has weakened. Distinctiveness measures surprise against the words a phrase is made of, and the two readings are made of different words: ἠμί and δέ are commoner than the exact shapes ἦν and δ’, so a phrase built from them is less surprising.

Reading the list

  • Distinctiveness orders the list by default. It asks whether a phrase occurs more often than the commonness of its words would lead you to expect. Sort by plain frequency instead and you see the grammar of Greek rather than the thought of Plato.
  • Filters narrow the list by phrase length, by how often a phrase occurs, and by which dialogue it appears in. Sorting never removes anything — it only changes the order.
  • The English list indexes the translation each dialogue carries, which here is the Loeb throughout — 4 translators working to one editorial standard rather than a different hand for every dialogue. So an English phrase that recurs across several dialogues is more likely to be Plato recurring than one translator's habit, and the Works column is the thing to read. It is still English: a phrase in this list is a place to look, not evidence about the Greek.

Click any phrase to see every place it occurs, grouped by dialogue, each one linked to that passage in the reader.

A way to use it

Pick the dialogue you are reading and a phrase length of four or five, then sort by distinctiveness. You get that dialogue's set expressions — often the working vocabulary of its argument, including phrases no glossary would have sent you to look for. Click one to see where else in Plato it stands.

ΘWhat these tools cannot tell you

All of this depends on a word-by-word grammatical description of the Greek, and that description is often uncertain. Where it is, these tools say so rather than choose an answer and present it as fact. The limits worth knowing:

  • A result marked as having several possible readings comes from a form the description cannot settle. Of the 7,212 optatives, 4,054 are settled and the rest are not.
  • Searches ignore accents, so λόγος and λογός count as one word. The search page has a setting to match accents exactly. Of the 46,138 spellings the corpus knows, 6,730 belong to more than one dictionary word, and no accent-blind search can separate them.
  • The phrase index shows Greek without accents, because it does not record them. We do not guess them.
  • Where a speech begins is worked out from the text, and in a few places only to the nearest line of Greek rather than to the exact word. Any search that turns on speaker turns says so when those dialogues are involved.
  • An exact phrase search cannot match across the boundary between two Stephanus sections; the phrase index and the proximity search can. So the index will sometimes count a phrase once more than an exact search finds it.
  • None of these tools reads Greek. They match forms and count them. What a passage means remains your work.

The Greek text is the TLG. The grammatical descriptions come from Morpheus, by way of Diogenes; the dictionary is Liddell–Scott–Jones.
