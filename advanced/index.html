A guide

How search works

Tools for asking questions of Plato's Greek, and of its translations, that a plain word search cannot answer. You need no Greek for most of them, and you can read the sections in any order. Every number on this page is counted from the texts this site serves: 36 works, 561,531 Greek words. Check any of them against the search itself.

First, three things worth knowing

Greek changes the ends of words. English mostly does not: "virtue" looks the same wherever it stands. Greek changes the ending to show the word's job, so ἀρετή, ἀρετῆς, and ἀρετήν are one word doing three jobs. A search for one spelling would miss most of the places the word occurs. That is why Form starts at Any form of this word: type any form, and the search finds them all.

The search reads every work. In starts at all works: all 36, including the 10 marked dubious (Alcibiades I, Alcibiades II, Hipparchus, Lovers, Theages, Hippias Major, Clitophon, Minos, Epinomis, and Letters). Set In to authentic works for the other 26, or choose works… to pick your own.

You can type Greek without a Greek keyboard. Type texnh and the search reads τέχνη. Typing Greek has the details.

Numbers like 34b are Stephanus pages and sections, the standard way of citing Plato. Most editions print them in the margin, so the same number finds the same passage in any edition or translation. Results here cite by page and section, never by line, and the search counts sections: each one with a hit counts once.

ΑTyping Greek

Use this when you have no Greek keyboard.

Beta Code writes Greek in Latin letters, one Latin letter for each Greek one, with marks written after the letter they belong to: a)reth/ is ἀρετή, and texnh is τέχνη. Scholars have long used it to put Greek into computers, and the Greek box reads it without being asked. h is η and w is ω, so e and o stay short. q is θ, c is ξ, and x is χ. A final s becomes ς by itself.

Beta Code, beside the Greek box, turns your letters into Greek as you type: a)reth/ becomes ἀρετή in the box, so you can see what you asked for. With it off, the box keeps your Latin letters and the search reads them the same way. Beta Code help, under Try on the search page, lists every letter and mark.

Keyboard, also beside the Greek box, opens a Greek keyboard on the screen. Type a vowel, then press a mark, and the mark joins the vowel.

You may leave the marks off: the search ignores accents, breathings, and capitals. Turn on Accents, under the Greek box, to match the marks exactly. Then λόγος and λογός are different searches, and a word typed without marks finds only words printed without them.

ΒWildcards

Use this when you know how a word begins but not how it ends.

End a word with * and it stands for whatever follows. With Form set to Only as I typed it, fron* reaches 68 different spellings across the works: φρόνησις, φρόνιμος, φρονεῖν, and the rest of that family at once. Since Greek marks a word's job at the end, one wildcard gathers the whole paradigm. Under Any form of this word, the search goes further and adds every other form of the words those spellings belong to.

A wildcard reaches further the more text you give it. In the Republic alone, l* reaches 302 different spellings; across all 36 works it reaches 891, out of 46,389 in the corpus. The search counts places, not spellings, so it answers l* with passages, not with a word count.

A star can also stand inside a word, and ? stands for exactly one letter. l*s is every word that begins with λ and ends with ς, 228 different spellings. log?s finds λόγος but not λόγους, which has two letters in that place. The pattern must match the whole word, so a star in the middle never quietly becomes "every word beginning λ".

Greek also takes . for one letter, + for one or more of the letter before, [ ] for one of several letters ([^ ] for any letter but those), ( | ) for either of two, and ^ and $ for the start and end of the word. (φιλ|σοφ)ια finds both φιλία and σοφία.

A star at the start of a word depends on how you type it. In Greek letters it is a wildcard: *ικος finds every word ending in -ικος. In Beta Code a star before a letter makes a capital, so there you write the ending as .*ikos$. Beta Code also reads ( ) | + as marks, so groups and alternatives need Greek letters. In English a leading star is always a wildcard: *ness finds happiness and goodness.

Running text

Set Form to Running text and the search reads the Greek as one stream of letters, across the breaks between words. λογ finds λόγος and ἀναλογία: the letters wherever they stand, at the start of a word or inside one. Here . is one letter, .* any run of letters, and | either of two. Each break between words counts as one space, so you can search across words too. Turn on Punctuation to match the stops as well: , . ; and ·.

ΓLemma

Use this when you want one dictionary word, in every form, and nothing else.

Any form of this word already works out the dictionary word from whatever form you type. Lemma is for when you would rather choose it yourself. Turn on Lemma, under the Greek box, and the box asks for a dictionary word. Start typing, and a list of Dictionary words opens: the headword, a short meaning, and how often it occurs. Pick the one you mean. The search then finds every form of that word and no other: no spelling that merely looks like it, and no other word that one of its forms could also belong to.

Typing ψυχ offers ψυχή first, as the commonest, then the other words that begin that way, such as ψυχρός, so you choose rather than guess. The list holds 12,900 entries, one for every word the search can find. A term set to any form in Proximity picks from the same list.

The search ignores accents, so words that differ only by an accent, such as ὅρος and ὄρος, are one entry in the list, and the list says that it cannot separate them.

ΔWord endings

Use this when you know a phrase but want every form of it, not just the one you typed.

An exact phrase search finds only the version you typed. Search two or more Greek words and a button appears under the count: Find this phrase in any inflection. It works out which dictionary word each of your words belongs to, then looks for every form of those words standing in the order you gave.

The phrase index shows how much that gathers. τὸ καλόν ("the beautiful") stands 93 times as those exact letters, in 14 works. Read as dictionary words, ὁ καλός, which gathers τοῦ καλοῦ, τῷ καλῷ, τῶν καλῶν, and the rest, it stands 385 times, in 32. To find those yourself you would have to know that τό, τοῦ, and τῷ are one word. The tool knows it for you.

It invents nothing. πάνυ μὲν οὖν ("certainly") stands 333 times as written and 333 times read as dictionary words: those three words take one form only, so there is nothing to gather. A tool that always found more would tell you nothing.

ΕEnglish searches

Use this to find a word in the translations, in any of its forms.

Type in the English box. An English search finds a word in all its forms by default: a plural or possessive finds its singular, and back. In the translations here "soul" stands 1,073 times, and "souls" and "soul's" 198 times more. Form set to Any form of this word finds them all; Only as I typed it finds the one spelling you gave. Irregular plurals are known too: "man" finds "men", "statesman" finds "statesmen".

Match works as it does for Greek. All words wants every word somewhere in the passage, Any word at least one of them, and Exact phrase the words in a row, in your order. A phrase is matched as whole words, so "the good" does not find "they" or "goodness". A hyphenated word, "self-control", is found as its words standing together, and a word printed with an accent is found when you type plain letters.

Every translation

The indexed English is the Loeb translation, by 4 translators across the works. 12 works also carry Benjamin Jowett's translation. An English search looks in every translation of a work, and asks each separately. A passage that several translations match is one hit: the Greek once, and one translation's English. Under it stand the names of the translators whose words matched. Click a name to read that translation of the passage instead.

Works with hits, beside the results, shows each work with its translations under it, and counts the sections in each. Choose a work or a translator to see only its hits, and choose it again to see them all. A Jowett passage is placed at the section where its speech begins, since his translation is matched to Plato's speeches, not to the Stephanus sections.

Greek and English at once

Type in both boxes and a switch appears between them, Hits match, with three settings: and, and/or, and or. Each counts sections.

  • and (Both languages) keeps a section only if it holds both terms: your Greek and your English.
  • and/or (Either or both) keeps a section with either term or both. A new search opens on this setting.
  • or (One language, not both) keeps a section only if one language has hits and the other has none. It is the exclusive or.

Under the count, a search in both boxes lists three rows, Greek and English, Greek only, and English only, each with its number of sections. The rows that the switch counts are the ones lit.

A link to a Greek and English search written before the switch had three settings carries no setting, and still means and.

ϚSearch by grammar

Use this to find a kind of word rather than a particular word.

Every Greek word here carries a grammatical description: its case, number, tense, and so on. You can search those descriptions instead of the spelling. In Proximity, set a term's Kind to by grammar. Choose a word class: a declined word, a verb, or a class that takes neither case nor tense, such as a particle or a preposition. Then rows open for what that class has. A declined word has Case, Number, Gender, Degree, and Part of speech (noun, adjective, article, or pronoun); a verb has Tense, Mood, Voice, Person, and Number, and a participle adds Case, Number, and Gender. Pick a value in as many rows as you like; a word must match them all. You need not know how any of these forms are spelled.

The part of speech is the class that LSJ gives the word, where it gives one. Where it does not, the Cambridge Greek Lexicon decides, and then Morpheus. Particles follow Smyth: an adverb, a conjunction, or both, as his grammar classes them.

Grammar searched across all of Plato would not be a question: there are 19,615 genitive plural feminines in the works, and a list of them tells you about Greek rather than about Plato. So a grammar term runs on its own only once something narrows it: fewer works under In, a speaker, or a word near or not near it. Ask without any, and the page offers Choose works, Choose speakers, and Add a word near or not near instead of results. Aorist passive participles in the Phaedo is a question; a word near a verb in the optative is another.

Grammar also sorts results you already have. Set Group by to By grammar and the hits fall into groups by their analysis, with a Grammar list beside them: tick a case or a tense there to keep only those hits.

Why some results say "one of several readings"

The same Greek letters can be two different forms. When that happens the hit shows what is left open, rather than choosing for you.

Plato's works hold 7,202 optatives. Of those, 4,054 have one possible reading; a hit on any of the rest comes back marked, because you could read those letters another way. The mark shows no flaw in the search. It reports a fact about the Greek.

A clearer case: of the 473 genitive duals (the dual is the form for pairs), not one is certain. In the dual, genitive and dative share one ending. The search cannot tell them apart because Greek does not, and it says so instead of deciding for you.

ΖProximity

Use this to find where two ideas are discussed together.

Proximity finds passages where two or more terms stand close together. Press Proximity, beside Search, to open the editor. Search either word alone and you get every passage that holds it; ask for both together and you get the passages where Plato is doing something with the pair. ἀρετή near ἐπιστήμη is one such question.

The editor

  • Kind: what a term is. any form is a dictionary word in every form, picked from the list (see Lemma); as typed is one spelling; exact phrase is several words in a row, such as ὦ Σώκρατες; by grammar is a description (see Search by grammar).
  • Distance, on Term 1's row: how close is close. Within 5 words at first; you can set it as high as 50, or choose the same section.
  • Relation, on every term after the first: near, before, after, or not near, and the term it is measured from. Usually that is Term 1, but it can be any earlier term.
  • and or or, before each term after the first. A term joined by or stands in for the one before it, and either will do: either ἡδονή or λύπη near ἀρετή. It takes that term's relation, and its row says so.
  • Add term and Remove. A search holds up to four terms.
  • In this order: every term must appear in the order you gave. Once any term says before or after, those settle the order, and the switch stays on.
  • Keep hits that cross from one speaker to the next: on at first, since a question and its answer belong together. Turn it off, and a window must stay inside one speech. The results then carry a Within one speech chip, which undoes the setting (see Who is speaking).

Each term must fall within the distance of the term it is measured from. The distance is counted between those two words alone, not across the whole group, so three terms within 10 words can span more than 10 words in all. Not near turns that round: the passage must have no such word within the distance of its term. ἡδονή before ἀρετή, with λύπη not near asks for pleasure without pain.

Once a proximity search has run, it reads as one sentence above the editor, with Turn off, Close, and Guide under it (Change in place of Close when the editor is shut). A change in the editor is not searched until you press its Search.

A match never crosses from one book into the next, however wide the distance: the last words of one book and the first of the next are not near each other in any useful sense.

ΗWho is speaking

Use this to hear one voice at a time.

Plato's dialogues name their speakers, and the Greek text marks each change of speaker. So a search can be held to one mouth: ἀρετή as Socrates uses it, and separately as everyone else does. Under the form, a Spoken by row, laid out like the In row, offers anyone and choose speakers…. Choose speakers, then pick Only these speakers or Anyone but these, and tick the names. The cast offered is read from the works you have selected, most talkative first, with the number of speeches beside each name. The filter applies to every kind of search on the page, and each Greek hit is labelled with its speaker.

Across the 25 works that carry speaker labels there are 11,827 speeches by 43 named speakers; 3,924 of those speeches are Socrates'.

What it holds to, and what it cannot

  • A Greek hit is held to the word: it is kept only if the speech it stands in belongs to a chosen speaker.
  • An English hit is held to the passage. The translation is aligned to the Stephanus section, not to the word, so an English match is kept when someone you chose speaks anywhere in that section. Expect the English side to be a little generous.
  • In Proximity, every term must fall in a chosen speaker's speech. By default a window may run from one speech into the next. Turn off Keep hits that cross from one speaker to the next and it must stay inside one speech. On a phone, the Filter list shows Within one speech under This search, with a × that turns the switch back on.
  • Some works (Apology, Charmides, Letters, Lovers, and Republic) carry no speaker labels in the Greek text. Where a narrator reports who spoke inside the prose, as in the Republic, nothing in the narration can be attributed to a speaker. A filtered search leaves these works out and says so under the results. Anyone but Socrates does not admit them either: a word nobody is marked as saying is not known to be someone else's.
  • The reported dialogues (Euthydemus (Socrates), Lysis (Socrates), Parmenides (Cephalus), Phaedo (Phaedo), Protagoras (Socrates), and Symposium (Apollodorus)) are labelled only at the frame — the narrator and whoever asked for the story — so every word of the conversation inside would count as the narrator's. They are left out too, and named under the results.
  • Words before a work's first speaker label have no speaker and fail both modes.

The speaker names are the ones the reader prints beside each turn, taken from the OCT sigla. Where a translation names a speaker differently ("Meno's Boy" for ΠΑΙ.), it is the reader's name that appears here.

ΘReading the results

Use this to make a long list of hits answer your question.

The bar over the results reads, for example, "343 sections · 463 hits". The first number counts Stephanus sections: each section with a hit counts once, however many hits it holds. The second counts hits where a hit is one thing, a Greek word, or an English word in a search where no work answers in more than one translation. Where several translations answer, the bar gives sections alone, since a hit count would add up translators. Each result shows its Stephanus page and section, the Greek with the match marked, and the English under it. Click a Greek word in a hit to see its dictionary entry: the headword, its meaning, how the form parses, and how often it appears in Plato.

  • Group by: By work, with a heading for each; By form, each spelling apart; or By grammar. A search offers only the groupings that fit it.
  • Sort: Stephanus order, Works A–Z, Forms A–Z, or Commonest form first. A sort orders the page you are on; it never moves a hit to another page.
  • Context: 1 line, 3 lines, or the Whole section each hit stands in.
  • Per page: 20, 40, or 100 hits. The 100 page shows each hit as its citation and the words around the first match; click one to open the passage.

Each page holds the number of rows chosen under Per page, so a section with many rows can run on to the next page.

Beside the results, Works with hits counts the sections in each work, most first ("63 sect."). Translations counts the English hits in each translation. Forms lists every spelling the search found, with its count: untick the ones you do not want and press Apply. Phrases lists the English phrases around the hits and sets any of them aside. Export results as CSV gives you the whole list as a spreadsheet. Save search keeps the search in this browser, and Saved searches lists them.

A search is written into the address bar as you run it, so you can bookmark it, send it to someone, or step back through your searches with the browser's back button.

ΙThe phrase index

Use this when you do not yet know what to look for.

Search answers "where does this occur?" The phrase index answers a different question: what does Plato repeat? It lists every phrase of two to five words that appears at least twice anywhere in the works: 84,894 of them as written, 219,237 when forms of the same word count together, and 187,170 in the English translations. (If you have met the term, these are what linguists call n-grams.)

This matters because a dialogue is built as much out of set phrases as out of single words, and no word search will show them to you: ἦν δ’ ἐγώ ("said I", Socrates narrating) occurs 860 times across 11 works; πάνυ μὲν οὖν ("certainly") 333 times; ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν ("so to speak") 74 times in 20. Not one of those is a word. Each is a habit of thought, and browsing is the only way to meet them.

Reading a row

Every row is one phrase and four numbers. This is the row for ἦν δ’ ἐγώ:

The same phrase, counted two ways

The As written and Dictionary words buttons at the top of the page read the same corpus two ways, and it is worth seeing what changes. As written counts the letters on the page: change one ending and it is a different row. Dictionary words counts the words behind the letters, so every inflection of the phrase gathers into a single row. Either takes the phrase as it stands on the page: type ἦν δ’ ἐγώ and the second finds it under ἠμί δέ ἐγώ, which is where it is stored, and tells you it read the words that way. English does the same for the translations.

The dictionary row is never the smaller of the two: it gathers the formula's other endings in with it. It also asks more of you: the same dictionary words can be assembled into phrases that are not the one you wanted, and the tool cannot tell the difference. Use it to discover what you would otherwise miss, then read as written to see what you have.

The score changes between the two rows for what is, in the main, the same expression. Nothing has weakened. Distinctiveness measures surprise against the words a phrase is made of, and the two readings are made of different words: ἠμί and δέ are commoner than the exact shapes ἦν and δ’, so a phrase built from them is less surprising.

Reading the list

  • Sort starts at distinctiveness. It asks whether a phrase occurs more often than the commonness of its words would lead you to expect. Sort by frequency instead and you see the grammar of Greek rather than the thought of Plato.
  • Length, At least … times, and In narrow the list by phrase length, by how often a phrase occurs, and by which work it appears in. A box for the start of a phrase finds one by its first words. Sorting never removes anything; it only changes the order.
  • The English list indexes the Loeb translation of each work — 4 translators working to one editorial standard rather than a different hand for every work. So an English phrase that recurs across several works is more likely to be Plato recurring than one translator's habit, and the Works column is the thing to read. It is still English: a phrase in this list is a place to look, not evidence about the Greek.

Click any phrase to see every place it occurs, grouped by work, each one linked to that passage in the reader.

A way to use it

Pick the work you are reading and a phrase length of four or five, then sort by distinctiveness. You get that work's set expressions, often the working vocabulary of its argument, including phrases no glossary would have sent you to look for. Click one to see where else in Plato it stands.

ΙΑWhat these tools cannot tell you

All of this depends on a word-by-word grammatical description of the Greek, and that description is often uncertain. Where it is, these tools say so rather than choose an answer and present it as fact. The limits worth knowing:

  • A result marked as having several possible readings comes from a form the description cannot settle. Of the 7,202 optatives, 4,054 are settled and the rest are not.
  • Searches ignore accents unless you turn on Accents. And of the 46,138 spellings the corpus knows, 6,742 belong to more than one dictionary word, so a search for one word in all its forms can bring back another word that shares a spelling.
  • The phrase index shows Greek without accents, because it does not record them. We do not guess them.
  • Where a speech begins is worked out from the text, and in a few places only to the nearest line of Greek rather than to the exact word. Any search that turns on speaker turns says so when those works are involved.
  • An exact phrase search cannot match across the boundary between two Stephanus sections; the phrase index and the proximity search can. So the index will sometimes count a phrase once more than an exact search finds it.
  • None of these tools reads Greek. They match forms and count them. What a passage means remains your work.

The Greek text is the TLG. The grammatical descriptions come from Morpheus, by way of Diogenes; the dictionary is Liddell–Scott–Jones.
